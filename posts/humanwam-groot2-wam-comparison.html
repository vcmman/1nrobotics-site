From HumanWAM to GR00T 2: world-action models, data, and deployment

A technical comparison of predictive architectures, human and UMI data, camera alignment, benchmark protocols, and the post-training needed to make a world-action model work on a real robot.

November 12, 2026 · 34 min read · 1N Robotics

Companion analysis · Why agentic robotics is accelerating: NVIDIA's loops and TinyAstral's RSI

A connector misses its socket by a few millimetres. The language instruction was understood; the grasp looked sensible. The remaining problem is how the scene will change as the hand approaches, and whether the next action can correct that change. World-action models try to learn this relationship. Their industrial value will depend on how cheaply it can be adapted and closed into a reliable feedback loop.

HumanWAM and GR00T 2 approach that problem from different experimental priorities. HumanWAM makes data alignment, human pretraining, and visual goals unusually inspectable. GR00T 2 exposes more of the path from mixed pretraining to embodiment adaptation, reinforcement learning, and an agent-assisted runtime. We compare those choices, rather than treating a larger corpus or a higher percentage as an answer by itself. [1] [3] [4]

Evidence is frozen at 12 November 2026: the GR00T 2 whitepaper and Open-HumanWAM v2 are the principal references. “Reported” below means author-reported results, not our reproduction. Engineering recommendations and the final outlook are our interpretation. A missing result means no matched result was established from the reviewed sources, not a zero score. [2] [3]

1. What changes when a policy also predicts the world?

A conventional vision-language-action policy models actions from observations, language, and state. A WAM additionally learns a future-world target. In a generative WAM, actions and future observations can share a conditional distribution. This changes the supervision: successful actions must agree with a plausible transition, rather than merely resemble a demonstration. It does not guarantee a causally correct simulator, force estimation, or safe exploration.

VLA: p(A | O, L, s)
WAM: p(O+, A | O, L, s, g?)
L = λvideo Lvideo + λaction Laction

Here O is observed history, O+ a future representation, A an action chunk, s proprioception, and g an optional goal. The loss is a schematic description, not a shared implementation: attention masks, noise schedules, and target representations determine whether future prediction actually informs action. Counting both losses is insufficient. A useful ablation removes future-to-action information while keeping training budget fixed.

DreamZero established an important generative reference: a 14B video-action model with reported 7Hz control after substantial optimization. That is not a latency guarantee for an arbitrary GPU; its released runtime documents different configurations. EgoWAM asks a complementary question under controlled co-training: are pixels even the best world target? Its DINO and camera-stabilized 3D-flow results motivate testing task-relevant representation, rather than equating photorealism with physical understanding. [11] [12] [18]

Two unrelated projects use the name OpenWAM. We call the 6,400-hour modular pretraining model OpenWAM-α. Separately, Stanford's composable framework compares joint, video-first, action-first, and decoupled programs on a shared setup. Its LIBERO results are useful counterevidence to an absolute claim that joint denoising always wins. The model identity and protocol must travel with every number. [15] [17] [25]

2. GR00T 2 versus HumanWAM: compare three configurations

“HumanWAM” is not a single architecture. Vanilla uses a Wan2.2-TI2V-5B video expert plus an action expert. The final Vanilla policy uses inverse-dynamics attention and video-then-action inference; the isolated-attention scaling experiments can skip future video at execution. Goal switches to a Cosmos3-Nano-16B backbone and a shared generator. Mixing these configurations would attribute changes in architecture and data to visual goals alone. [3] [26] [27]

Parameter labels require a denominator. GR00T 2's quoted 2B describes its shared transformer, not the entire sensor-to-actuator system. The whitepaper's tested Turbo configurations also use 2B; their efficiency gain includes video compression and runtime changes. HumanWAM's 5B and 16B labels describe different backbones, not matched counts of trainable policy parameters. None of these labels normalizes camera tokens, reasoning encoders, denoising steps, or hardware. [2] [3]

Our technical reading is that the choice of action interface matters more than the parameter headline. A common EEF space makes demonstrations comparable, but hides joint limits, finger contacts, and compliance. A body-specific decoder preserves those details but moves work into adaptation. Neither approach removes embodiment: it chooses where to pay for it.

The Human-1M release card preserves timestamps, rig identity, calibration, and source metadata. Open-HumanWAM preserves clip manifests and provenance; its 50K hours are a selected corpus, not 35.9 million independent physical trials. These details make a scaling result auditable. For procurement, cost per accepted, usable interaction is more meaningful than camera-on hours. Neither project provides a complete comparable dollar-per-hour acquisition bill. [5] [7]

4. Head and wrist views: alignment before camera count

The head camera preserves workspace context, object relations, and task intent. A wrist camera makes the grasp site larger in the image and can see contact hidden by the other arm. It also moves with the effector, narrows the field of view, and can lose the destination during transport. Head + wrist is therefore a coverage choice with calibration and token costs, not automatically a stronger model.

HumanWAM supplies a particularly useful negative result. In its isolated-attention ablation, adding wrists only during robot post-training damages scene-OOD and random performance. In the IDM setting, EgoPro mid-training partly repairs the transition; observing all views while predicting only the head view is better than predicting every view. These are matched ablations inside that study, not evidence that all multi-view policies fail. [3]

Synchronization has a physical unit. As an illustrative calculation, 20ms of image–action skew at 0.25m/s means 5mm of displacement, already larger than many insertion tolerances. A collection pipeline should record exposure and state timestamps, estimate offsets and drift, preserve camera-to-effector transforms, and verify alignment during motion. Matching nominal frame rates is insufficient; rolling shutter and buffering can still shift the contact event.

Generalist's data strategy is relevant because GEN-1.5 physical prompts normally contain sensorimotor examples from handheld grippers or robot rollouts. We use “wrist-first” as an engineering interpretation: prioritize a consistent near-hand observation–action interface. The reviewed official posts do not establish an exclusively wrist-only camera stack, an all-else-equal comparison against head + wrist, or a disclosed WAM video-generation objective. GEN-1.5 is an important adaptation reference, not a confirmed architectural twin of GR00T 2. [19] [20]

5. Visual goals, video prompts, and gradient updates are different adaptation tools

A goal image specifies a state. A video specifies transitions and ordering. A sensorimotor prompt additionally supplies actions. This hierarchy matters when the desired final picture is compatible with several incompatible procedures: a closed lid does not say whether to screw, press, or latch it. HumanWAM-Goal's outcome interface can help an agent communicate an intermediate target, but a selected goal-following clip does not prove procedure learning from arbitrary human video. [4]

Zero-WAM explicitly trains use of human-video context. HumanGen generates semantically paired human videos from robot trajectories, and the in-context future-chunk objective discourages shortcuts. The reported 46.95% is on seven held-out RoboTwin tasks, not the 50-task post-training suite. This reduces one task-specification gap while introducing another audit problem: generated human videos must preserve the task and its physical ordering. [13] [14]

GEN-1.5 reports 59% ±10% one-shot ICL and 83% ±9% after ten gradient steps using five minutes per task, across ten short-horizon tasks; the reported uncertainties are standard deviations, not confidence intervals. Its 100Hz action trajectories are not a measured 10ms full-model inference latency. “No updates,” “a few updates,” and “zero target-robot task demonstrations” answer different questions, and should be three separate columns in any adaptation claim. [19]

6. Benchmarks: keep the embodiment and denominator attached

RoboTwin 2.0 evaluates bimanual Aloha-AgileX tabletop manipulation; Clean2Random introduces visual/environment variation after clean-demonstration training. RoboDojo probes bimanual generalization, precision, long horizon, memory, and open instructions; progress and complete success are separate metrics. LIBERO's four suites are not a replacement for either. RoboCasa365 has 365 tasks in its environment collection, while the cited leaderboard experiment evaluates a 50-task selection, not 365 task successes averaged together. [21] [22] [23] [24] [2]

7. Post-training: where GR00T 2 closes the contact gap

Robot SFT grounds a broad model in the target machine's camera, state, and action distribution. LoRA lowers the cost of updating the transformer, not the cost of collecting trustworthy examples. For Dexter-50, the report uses rank-32 adapters, one or five task episodes, and roughly 30 minutes on one RTX 5090 per task. Frozen encoders and heads still require compatible observations and normalizations. This is efficient adaptation under a defined interface, not plug-and-play control of an unknown body. [2]

Can UMI replace a target robot's same-task demonstrations? In the reported UMI→YAM experiments, yes, conditionally: UMI supplies task behavior while YAM contributes task-agnostic data. Shared action heads, grasp-site transforms, aperture normalization, and YAM-aligned wrist rendering bridge the gaps. Toy-truck assembly reaches 60% with aligned views but 0% without warping in the reported ablation. The result removes one demonstration source; it does not remove the target body's calibration, play data, controller, or validation. [1] [2] [10]

The three RL options spend interaction differently. Group-relative RL changes the base WAM, using stochastic action-denoising transitions and reward comparisons; its context-history baseline retains information across rounds. Residual RL freezes the base and learns bounded corrections, making rollback and local specialization easier. Self-imitation collects successful RL-discovered episodes and starts again from the SFT initialization with supervised video/action losses. It inherits exploration cost even when the consolidation phase needs no new rollouts. [2]

The report uses 1.5K simulated episodes per task. DreamDojo neural-simulation RL is a separate path and needs recorded task rollouts to build its environment. A generated future inside a policy is not automatically an environment calibrated well enough for policy optimization. [1] [2]

Latency is not the inverse of action sampling rate. A 1.6-second trajectory can contain many high-rate setpoints yet be refreshed only once per second. Camera exposure, encoding, network transport, queueing, denoising, and controller tracking all contribute to response. Report p50 and p95 sensor-to-command age under load, missed deadlines, and recovery after stale chunks. Asynchronous serving needs prefix alignment or another explicit continuity contract.

Reproduction should pin the checkpoint, dataset manifest, per-body action convention, calibration, normalization statistics, camera ordering, denoising schedule, simulator version, and evaluation seeds. Publishing a repository is only the beginning. HumanWAM's source datasets retain their original terms; a wrapper's Apache license does not relicense them. GR00T's published human-data subset is not the complete training mixture. Full pretraining resource disclosure is also uneven: GR00T reports 1,024 GB300 GPUs, whereas no like-for-like full training bill was established for HumanWAM from the reviewed release. [2] [5] [6] [7] [25]

Our proposed deployment sequence is deliberately testable: calibrate and replay recorded motions; establish a robot-SFT baseline; test task-agnostic and same-task UMI transfer separately; add RL only to a measured failure mode; then add agent recovery with an explicit retry and time budget. Freeze a holdout set before each step. Promote a change only if complete-task success improves without an unacceptable increase in cycle time, intervention, damage, or compute cost.

9. What deserves attention over the next one to two years?

Our first priority is accepted-interaction scaling. More human hours are useful when they add new contacts, objects, procedures, and recoveries after curation. Hold robot data and compute fixed, then test unseen scenes and tasks. Report the marginal acquisition and processing cost. Otherwise a scaling curve can conceal better sampling or a larger adaptation budget.

Second, evaluate unified actions as a hierarchy. EEF trajectories are a useful shared geometric interface; dexterous contacts and whole-body balance may need body-specific residuals, force targets, or motion tokens. A useful cross-body test holds the task fixed, counts every target-body calibration and play episode, and measures how much of the prior survives without re-learning contact from scratch.

Third, compare wrist-only, head-only, and combined rigs under equal accepted task hours and model-token budgets. Measure occlusion failure, destination loss, recalibration, and transfer to a different hand. We expect wrist-first interfaces to be attractive for bounded contact work, and wider views to remain useful for task composition and navigation. This is a falsifiable design hypothesis, not a winner established by the existing cross-paper scores.

Fourth, reduce future prediction to what control needs. Sparse visual futures, latent dynamics, asynchronous updates, and task-dependent denoising could improve reaction cost. The acceptance test is physical success at matched latency, including precision and perturbation recovery, with an action-only ablation. A fast model that loses the failure signal is not a successful compression.

Finally, WAM + RL + Agent should be evaluated as an accountable system. The WAM proposes a physically grounded action chunk; RL improves a repeatable contact error; the agent detects a broken precondition, changes a subgoal, or retrieves a recovery. Each layer needs an observable contract and an ablation. Track complete-task reliability over repeated shifts, not only best-run success after unconstrained retries.

Our judgment: a promising training interface, an unfinished deployment contract

The strongest case for WAM is that broad human experience can supervise more than object semantics: it can constrain how intended motion changes a scene. HumanWAM makes that case legible through alignment, controlled views, and goals. GR00T 2 makes it operationally concrete through cross-adaptation, contact post-training, and runtime layering. Neither establishes that generated video is sufficient for reliable manipulation, or that an arbitrary new robot can skip grounding.

For a research group, follow HumanWAM when the question is what data or conditioning buys transfer; use matched ablations rather than its headline score. Follow GR00T 2 when designing the adaptation and evaluation stack, while accounting for resources and release scope. For deployment, start with one costly task, compare against a strong VLA under the same data and latency budget, and require evidence that predictive modeling reduces total cost at the required reliability. That is a more durable milestone than winning a mixed leaderboard.
